My side

I didn't ask him to cut it down. I said the plums were coming over, which they were, and that when you have pale paving you can't simply leave fruit on it, especially in August. He said he would come round with a bucket. I said there was no need for that. What I meant was there ought not to be a need for that, but he took me at my word, as he tends to do when it saves him a journey.

For the next fortnight I picked them up myself. Some went in the brown bin and some went into a bowl, depending on what had happened underneath, because a plum can look perfectly sound until you turn it over. I gave a few to my daughter. She said they were lovely. I said they weren't mine, and she said, well, whose are they, then, which was not really a question about ownership. She was eating one over the sink.

I used to have grass there. This is apparently relevant, because when I mentioned the marks to Brian he said, “Well, of course, when it was grass,” and let that stand as his contribution. Brian lives opposite. Nothing falls on his garden except leaves, which he blows into the road with a machine so loud that I cannot hear the radio in my kitchen. I have never raised this with him. I don't see what would be gained.

The tree was there when I moved in. I know that. So was the kitchen, but no one suggests I should still be putting up with those cupboards.

On the Sunday I went round to return the bowl. It was his bowl, a blue one from the winter when he brought me soup, and it had been on top of the fridge since March. I had washed it again because of the dust. He was in the side passage with a ladder, and I said I hoped he wasn't going up there by himself. He said his brother was coming. I said, “Well, mind,” which is something my mother used to say and which irritated me even as I said it.

He looked at the bowl as though he had never seen it before.

“Yours,” I said.

“Oh. Keep it.”

“I've brought it now.”

He took it by the rim. His other hand was holding the ladder, so there was nowhere for him to put it except on the ground, and I came away feeling that I had returned it at the wrong time, although there hadn't been an appointment.

They cut off the big branch on Tuesday. Not the whole tree. I could hear the saw while I was doing the accounts, and when I went upstairs the branch was already lying along his lawn with the little plums still on it. His brother had a rope over one shoulder. They were looking up at the rest.

It lets much more light into the back bedroom. I hadn't expected that. There is a rectangle on the wallpaper where the bookcase used to be, and the curtains are worse than I thought.

Yesterday he put a bag of plums on the wall. I took them in. There was no note, so I don't know whether I was supposed to say anything about the branch. I called across that I had the bag, but his kitchen door was shut.

I've made a crumble for when my daughter comes. There were more than I needed. The rest are in a saucepan with a little water; I'll decide about the sugar when they've softened.
