The storm cue

The first thunder was Martin's. He held the metal sheet by its cord and gave it the small, practiced shake that meant the estate was in danger but dinner could proceed.

Onstage, Lady Cresswell looked toward the French windows.

“We shall have weather before morning.”

The second thunder was not Martin's.

It lasted long enough for Lady Cresswell to look toward the French windows again. The windows were painted on plywood. Martin, who had painted them, had never been satisfied with the left-hand pane.

At the prompt desk, June put a line through something.

“Don't cross mine out,” Martin whispered.

“I'm marking where she is.”

“Oh.”

The real storm had arrived early. At the interval it was meant to be somewhere over the bypass.

Sir Robert entered with the letter. Behind him, in the passage to the fire door, water began falling into the bucket they used for this. It was already half full of brushes.

“I have received,” said Sir Robert, “a communication.”

A drop struck a brush handle. Martin moved toward the bucket.

June caught his sleeve and pointed to the script.

He had another thunder on “Calcutta.”

The letter was taking longer than usual. Sir Robert unfolded it, examined the blank side, and turned it over. He had insisted on having the words written out, although he knew them. Last night he had put on his glasses to read it, which had been felt to diminish the surprise.

Martin raised the sheet.

“Your brother,” Sir Robert said, “has been seen.”

The rain on the roof increased. In the front row someone coughed with great determination.

“In Calcutta.”

Martin shook.

At precisely the same moment the sky made a noise so large that Lady Cresswell said “Christ,” very quietly, but in the voice she used at the supermarket.

Sir Robert waited. Then he said, “Yes.”

There was a laugh from the audience. Not the usual laugh, which came later, when the brother's occupation was revealed.

June's pencil stopped.

Martin lowered the sheet. His cord had cut a red groove across his fingers.

For the next six pages there was no weather required. He went to the bucket, lifted out the brushes, and laid them on the towel under the fire extinguisher.

The bucket rang. He folded the end of the towel into it, and the ringing stopped.

When he got back, June held out half a barley sugar without looking at him. It had divided lengthways, which made it difficult to take.

Onstage, Lady Cresswell was refusing to sit down.

Martin sucked the sweet. Outside, the rain eased. You could hear the leading edge of it moving off the roof, a sudden shrinking of the room's noise.

June turned a page.

This was the difficult cue: a distant rumble beneath the revelation, not so loud that the audience missed the solicitor's name.

Martin took up the cord. He waited for “nevertheless.”

Nothing happened outside.

He gave the sheet a gentle ripple.
