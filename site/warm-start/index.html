Warm start

The dragon arrived folded inside a washing basket, with its tail through one of the handles.

“You said a small one,” said Ed.

“It is a small one.”

“For a dragon.”

“What other sort would I mean?”

June set the basket on the counter. The dragon opened an eye. It was the colour of a boiled sweet that had been under a sofa for some time.

They had forty minutes before the first customers arrived and no gas. Ed had already written COLD FOOD ONLY on the blackboard. Beneath it, in smaller letters, he had written Sorry, and beneath that June had added TODAY, because she felt his version suggested a permanent collapse.

“Does it know what to do?”

“It lives in a bakery.”

“That's not the same.”

“It'll know more than we do.”

The dragon unhooked its tail and climbed onto the counter. Its feet made a noise like someone sorting cutlery. It sniffed the little vase by the till and ate the daffodil.

June moved the other vase.

They put a frying pan in front of it. Ed cracked in two eggs, which spread slowly across the cold metal. The dragon watched with grave attention.

“Go on, then,” said June.

The dragon lay down.

“Perhaps it needs the command.”

“She didn't mention a command.”

“Who didn't?”

“Moira.”

“Ring Moira.”

“She's at a funeral.”

They stood looking at the eggs. A small bubble had appeared in one of the whites, but neither of them believed this was progress.

Ed tried “Fire,” then “Please,” then, with a glance at the window, “Ignition.”

The dragon closed its eye.

At eight, Mr Bell came in for his usual. June explained that there was a reduced menu.

“Toast, then.”

“The grill's gas as well.”

“Tea?”

“The kettle works.”

“Tea and toast.”

“We can't do the toast.”

Mr Bell looked past her at the dragon.

“Is that Moira's?”

“Yes.”

“What's it doing here?”

“Nothing,” said Ed.

Mr Bell hung his coat on its hook. He had been using that hook for eleven years. Once, when a tourist had hung a jacket there, he had eaten breakfast with his coat on.

“Lovely morning,” he said to the dragon.

It opened both eyes.

“Not for me. My sister's coming.”

The dragon raised its head.

“She'll want the spare room. I've got the trains in there now. Told her on the phone, there's trains in there, Margaret. She says, well, they can move.”

A thin blue flame slipped between the dragon's teeth. Ed reached for the pan.

“Sixty-four feet of track. Where am I moving that? Into the garden?”

The flame lengthened. Ed held the pan over it. The eggs began to spit.

“Last time she came, she put a mug down on the goods yard.”

June got a plate.

“On the signals.”

By half past eight they had served nine breakfasts. Mr Bell sat at the counter with his tea while June prompted him discreetly whenever the flame diminished. Parking. The new pharmacy. Whether a person could reasonably call a programme a documentary if it contained that much music.

At nine his sister walked in.

“There you are,” she said. “I've been ringing.”

Mr Bell stopped in the middle of an objection to decorative gravel. The dragon gave a small cough and went out.

Margaret took off her coat.

“Oh, don't get him started,” she told June. “I had forty minutes on the phone about a mug. I hadn't even put it down. I was holding it.”

The dragon turned towards her.

Ed put on another pan.
