Graham & Val

Portions

There was enough of Graham left for four people, or two hungry people who didn't want any more Graham tomorrow.

“Straight down the middle,” said Val.

“That gives you both ears.”

“How does that give me both ears?”

Dennis turned the box so she could see. Graham's photograph had been printed on a sheet of icing. At the party they had cut around his portrait, which everyone had found respectful until it was the only part remaining.

“I don't particularly want an ear,” Val said.

“Then you won't mind.”

The train had been standing outside Reading for twenty minutes. Beyond the window, a man in an orange jacket walked up the bank, stopped, and walked down again.

Dennis unwrapped a wooden fork from a napkin.

“You can't cut it with that.”

“I can make a line.”

“It's not a planning application. Just do it.”

He pushed the side of the fork into Graham's hair. The icing bent, but didn't break. Underneath it the sponge sank.

“You're making him longer,” said Val.

Dennis took the fork away. Graham slowly recovered some of his forehead.

“He didn't like that photograph.”

“He chose it.”

“He chose it for the website in 2008. He didn't choose to be a cake.”

Val took the fork. She made a row of holes from the parting to the knot of his tie, then went over them again. A small piece of blue tie came away on one of the prongs. She ate it.

“That was on my side.”

“You haven't got a side yet.”

Across the aisle, a woman lowered her book.

“Dental floss,” she said.

Dennis looked at her.

“For cutting. You hold a length tight.”

“Have you got any?” said Val.

“No.”

The woman raised her book again.

Dennis peeled back the box's front flap. With the fork and two fingers he pulled his portion towards him. The sponge split along Val's perforations, but the whole face came with it. For a moment Graham hung between the portions like a carpet being carried upstairs.

Val caught her half of the cake on a napkin.

“You can have him,” she said.

“I don't want all of him.”

“Well, I don't want you touching mine again.”

The announcement began with an apology. They stopped eating to hear it. It ended without a time.

“He'll still come in,” Dennis said.

“What?”

“Graham. He says he'll still come in on Fridays.”

Val chewed.

“He's kept his pass.”

“You're joking.”

“He said there's no point making someone come down to reception.”

The train moved about six feet and stopped. Val picked a crumb off her coat. Dennis was trying to fold the photograph small enough to eat, but each fold squeezed out a little more buttercream.

“Turn him over,” she said.

A few loose ends

Subject: Friday — not a meeting

Dear all,

Thank you again for yesterday. A wonderful send-off. I have put the cards on the mantelpiece and the clock in the kitchen, where it has already proved useful.

Several people asked what I would do with myself. This morning I went to the garden centre. It opens at nine, not half past eight as stated on the internet. I have let them know.

I shall pop in on Friday to return the cake knife. Please don't arrange anything. I am perfectly happy to make my own coffee, provided somebody can tell me whether the code is still 1964. No need to reply to everyone with this information.

There are a few things in the bottom drawer of my old desk which may be useful to whoever takes over. The short cable isn't faulty. It only works one way round. I can demonstrate on Friday.

I notice the calendar invitation has disappeared. That is quite right: I am no longer attending the weekly meeting. It would help to know when it finishes, however, so that my visit doesn't interrupt it. Allow perhaps ten minutes afterwards for the cable.

Val, did you and Dennis get home all right? I saw there were delays. I hope you had enough cake.

Best wishes, Graham

P.S. The knife is ours, not the office's, so I shall need to bring it home again.

Reception

At ten past eleven Graham rang Val from reception.

“It's me,” he said.

“I know. Your photograph's come up.”

“Which photograph?”

“The one you don't like.”

There was a pause.

“My pass isn't working.”

“I'll come down.”

“No hurry. I'm with—what's your name? Sorry, I can see your name. I'm with Sophie.”

When Val got downstairs, Graham was standing to one side of the desk with a carrier bag. Sophie had given him a visitor's badge.

“You'll need to sign him in,” she said.

“I worked here for thirty-one years,” Graham told Val.

“She started on Monday. You could both have missed each other.”

“I wasn't saying it like that.”

Val turned the book towards him. Under PURPOSE OF VISIT he wrote Returning knife.

Sophie read it upside down.

“Cake knife,” he added.

“It's fine,” she said, in a voice which suggested there was a procedure if it wasn't.

In the lift he held the bag apart for Val to look inside. The knife was wrapped in a tea towel. Beside it was a small plastic box.

“Did you bring more cake?”

“It's Sophie's.”

“She's given you cake?”

“She made banana bread. Nobody's had any.”

“It's only eleven.”

“She gets in at eight.”

He closed the bag.

“Apparently people don't stop down there.”

Upstairs, someone had put a fern on his desk.

Graham stopped beside it.

“Whose is that?”

“It's yours,” said Dennis.

“No, it isn't.”

“Your plant. You left it.”

Graham leaned down. The pot was familiar. He had never seen the plant from this side before.

“Oh,” he said. “Yes.”

“We turned it round.”

“I can see that.”

Dennis stood up, then looked at his chair, then at the chair behind Graham's old desk. Val took a third chair from the printer and put it at the end of the table.

“Sit there. Otherwise we'll be here all morning.”

Graham took off his coat. Underneath he was wearing a blue jumper neither of them had seen before.

“Coffee?” Dennis said.

“I can get it.”

“I'm getting one.”

“In that case, yes, please.”

Dennis went to the machine. Graham put the plastic box on the table and eased up one corner of the lid.

“Banana bread,” he said.

“We heard.”

“For everyone. Not just me.”

Val looked towards the lift.

“Is she on her own down there?”

“Till twelve.”

Val took her phone out.

“What are you doing?”

“Getting someone to cover reception.”

“Don't make a thing of it.”

“I'm not.”

“It was just a conversation.”

“Graham. Open the box.”

He opened it. Dennis came back carrying two coffees, with a packet of wooden stirrers held between his teeth.

“Have we got a knife?” he said.

Val looked at Graham's bag.

Graham moved it a little closer to his feet.

“Apparently,” he said, “there's a procedure.”

Cover

Nobody answered Val's first two calls. On the third, someone said they were working from home.

“Right,” she said, and went downstairs.

Sophie was eating a yoghurt behind the desk.

“Go up. They've made coffee.”

“I'm all right.”

“Take that with you.”

“It's not very good.”

“I wasn't asking for it.”

Sophie put the lid back on. She showed Val the button for the doors, the internal directory and the parcel book, then explained that the right-hand lift wasn't broken, although it sounded as though it was.

“I know,” said Val.

“Sorry. I keep telling people.”

“Tell them again. They might fix it.”

When she had gone, Val lowered the chair. It sank further than she intended. She stood up and pulled the lever. The chair rose until its armrests struck the desk.

The telephone rang.

“Good morning,” she said, still standing.

The caller wanted someone called Martin.

“Which Martin?”

“I don't know his surname.”

“Which department?”

“He wears glasses.”

Val looked at the directory. There were four Martins. She had worked with two of them and couldn't remember whether either wore glasses.

“What's it regarding?”

“His car.”

She put him through to the one she liked least and sat down carefully.

A few minutes later Graham came out of the lift carrying the fern. Its pot was in his carrier bag, but most of the plant wasn't.

“You're going, then,” said Val.

“I thought I'd better.”

“Did you demonstrate the cable?”

“They haven't got the thing it goes in any more.”

“Oh.”

“Dennis looked in the cupboard.”

He put the plant on the desk. One long frond settled across the visitor book.

“Is she having her cake?” Val asked.

“Yes. They're showing each other photographs of dogs.”

“She's got a dog?”

“Two.”

“You didn't stay?”

“I haven't got one.”

“Neither has Dennis. He borrows his sister's.”

Graham unfastened his visitor's badge. The clip caught in his jumper, and Val held out her hand for it before he'd freed it. She kept her hand there. Eventually he dropped it into her palm.

“Have you had lunch?” he asked.

“It's half past eleven.”

“I didn't say now.”

“I've got something upstairs.”

“What?”

“Something in a box.”

“Well, bring it.”

“Where?”

He looked through the glass doors. Across the road, two men were lifting a carpet out of a van.

“I hadn't got that far.”

Val's phone lit up beside the directory. She read the message and typed with one thumb.

“Dennis says you're gone.”

“I said goodbye.”

“He wants to know if you took the short cable.”

“Yes.”

Val typed again.

“He's found the thing.”

Graham closed his eyes.

“Tell him I'll bring it next week.”

“You're downstairs.”

“I know where I am.”

The desk phone rang. Val picked it up. After a moment she reached for the directory.

“Not that Martin. Right.”

Graham leaned towards her and pointed to a name. She waved his finger away, listened, then pulled the directory back under it.

“Thank you. I'll try him.”

She transferred the call.

“Used to have a red Volvo,” Graham said.

“Very useful.”

“You asked.”

“I didn't.”

“No.”

He lifted the plant off the visitor book. A little soil had fallen into the ruled space beneath his name. He brushed it into his hand.

“There are benches by the library,” Val said. “If it isn't wet.”

Graham looked at the trees beyond the van.

“I'll go and see.”

“You can leave that here.”

He put the fern back. Val moved it to the far end of the desk, where its leaves wouldn't catch the door button.

“Not there,” he said. “That's a terrible draught.”

“Go and find us a bench, Graham.”

Lunch

Graham had put his newspaper on the bench. When Val arrived, he was holding it down with both hands.

“It's dry,” he said.

“Then what's that for?”

“Birds.”

She looked up into the tree.

“Previous birds,” he said.

Val sat on the business section. Graham moved his hand just before she sat on it.

“Where's your plant?”

“Sophie likes it.”

“You've given it to her?”

“I'm thinking about it.”

He had bought a sandwich in a triangular box. Val took a round plastic tub from her bag and turned it over twice.

“What have you got?”

“I don't know.”

“You made it.”

“I made several things.”

She lifted the lid. They looked in.

“Is that lunch?” Graham said.

“It was going to be.”

Inside was a block of frozen tomato sauce. It had come away from the sides of the tub, leaving a white rim.

“You could take it back.”

“Then I'd have to explain.”

“To whom?”

“Everyone I told I was going for lunch.”

Graham opened his sandwich. It was cut into two unequal triangles, one of them mostly crust. He held the box towards her.

“No, eat your lunch.”

“I don't need all of it.”

“Graham.”

He withdrew the box.

They watched a woman wheel a bicycle towards the library doors. She leaned it against the wall, tried the doors, then read the notice on them. She looked at her watch and tried the doors again.

“Closed till one,” Graham said.

“Perhaps she knows something.”

The woman wheeled her bicycle away.

“She didn't,” he said.

Val put the tub on the bench between them.

“I might come in Monday,” Graham said.

“What for?”

He picked a piece of lettuce from the hinge of the box.

“Nothing in particular.”

Val rubbed a spot of condensation off the tub with her thumb.

“Monday's bad,” she said. “I've got the auditors.”

“I wouldn't need you.”

“I know.”

He took a bite of sandwich. A bus stopped on the other side of the road, hiding the office entrance. For a while they watched people get off it.

“There's a café in there,” he said, nodding towards the library.

“At one.”

“Yes.”

“I've got to be back.”

“Not today.”

“Oh.”

He folded the empty half of the sandwich box beneath the other half.

“Wednesday?” she said.

“Could do.”

“Don't put it in the office calendar.”

“I haven't got the office calendar.”

“No. Of course.”

Val took out her phone. Graham took out his. They spent a little time looking at separate screens.

“One?” he said.

“Half past. If it opens at one, you won't have to stand outside.”

“I don't always arrive early.”

She put her phone away.

“Give me the crusty one.”

He held out the box. She took the sandwich, and he caught the newspaper as it began to slide out from under her.

“Hold that,” he said.

She put the frozen sauce on it.
