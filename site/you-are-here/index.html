The man had brought his own ruler.

Sylvia watched him hold it against the map beside the ticket hut. Then he stepped backwards into the lavender.

“Mind the bees,” she said.

“This isn't to scale.”

“No.”

“It says it is.”

He pointed to the little striped bar beneath the lake. Sylvia had worked at the garden for nine years without noticing it. She disliked him for making her notice it now.

“I'll mention it,” she said.

“To whom?”

“Peter.”

“Is Peter responsible for the map?”

Peter was responsible for the mower, the roses, the lake when it was green, and most of the things Sylvia said she would mention.

“Yes.”

The man put his ruler in a leather sleeve.

“I've paid eight pounds.”

“And you can go all round.”

“That's rather the point.”

He unfolded a sheet of paper on the ticket counter. It was the garden, drawn in pencil, with measurements along the paths. His lake was a much less attractive shape than theirs.

“Did you do this today?”

“Yesterday. I came back.”

Another eight pounds. Sylvia revised her estimate of the seriousness of the situation.

“You've made the island very small,” she said.

“The island is very small.”

“There's a heron on it.”

“The heron isn't a unit of measurement.”

“It gives people an idea.”

“It gives people the idea there's a heron.”

There was not always a heron. Sylvia decided against introducing this difficulty.

Peter came through the gate carrying a length of hose. The man immediately spread his paper wider.

“Are you responsible for the map?”

“No,” said Peter.

Sylvia looked at him.

“My wife did it,” he said. “For the jubilee.”

“Then perhaps your wife—”

“She'd be delighted. She's been wanting it down for years.”

The man hesitated.

“The original was in watercolour,” Peter said. “They stretched it to fit the board. She said it made the lake look like a shoe.”

“It does,” said the man.

“She'll like you.”

Peter bent over the pencil drawing.

“You've got the old path.”

“I walked it.”

“Yes, but we don't send people that way. Wasps.”

The man looked down at his measurements.

“Seasonal,” he said.

“Very much so,” said Peter.

Sylvia took a blank event sticker from beneath the counter. She laid it neatly over the scale bar and wrote PLEASE DO NOT FEED THE DUCKS.

“There,” she said.

The man stared at it.

“That doesn't correct the distances.”

“I haven't got a sticker that does that.”

The man took out his pencil.

“There are several other things.”

Peter adjusted the hose on his shoulder.

“I'll leave you with Sylvia.”

“No, you won't,” she said.

At closing time they were still at the counter. Peter's wife had arrived with the original painting and a packet of custard creams. She and the man were getting on extremely well. They had agreed about the lettering and were now united against the car park.

Sylvia counted the till twice. It was eight pounds better off than it would have been without him.

She took three biscuits.
