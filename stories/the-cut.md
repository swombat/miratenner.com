# The cut


At the first rehearsal after his holiday, Martin sang into a silence which everyone else had evidently rehearsed.

He got through “and ever” before Elaine lowered both hands.

“Lovely,” she said. “That's gone.”

“What has?”

“That section. We cut it last week.”

Martin looked at the pencil bracket in Peter's score. It began halfway down one page and ended on the next.

“All of it?”

“We need to lose four minutes.”

Martin had taken his music to Portugal. He had sat on a balcony overlooking a swimming pool, learning the passage from a recording through headphones. His wife had said she could hear him from the pool. He had explained that the bass line was difficult to internalise without vocalising it, and she had asked whether it might be possible to internalise it after she finished swimming.

“That's the bit with the bottom E,” he said.

“Yes.”

“It's the only one.”

“We still have plenty of low notes.”

“Not that low.”

Elaine watched him find a pencil.

“It's a good cut, Martin. You'll see how it joins.”

They began four bars before the bracket. Peter leaned across at the crucial moment and pointed. Martin jumped from a plea for mercy to a statement of general confidence. It joined very well.

He disliked it immediately.

At the break he went to the urn. The tea bags were in a plastic tub marked ALTOS, a distinction whose original purpose nobody could now explain.

“Mine's gone too,” said Ruth.

“What?”

“My top A.”

He had forgotten that her ascent and his descent happened together. In the recording, the soprano had sounded ecstatic about it.

“It's a shame,” he said.

“It's a relief. I hate that A.”

He squeezed his tea bag against the side of the cup.

“You always get it.”

“Yes, but I have to get it every time.”

Ruth took two biscuits and went to sit by the radiator.

Martin stayed at the urn. Through the kitchen hatch he could see Elaine eating a banana while she turned pages with her free hand. She was wearing the same green jumper as before his holiday. There was a small hole at one elbow. He had spent eleven mornings on the passage.

He went over.

“Could we do it once?”

Elaine finished chewing.

“The cut?”

“Just once. Not in the concert.”

“Martin—”

“I know. But I learnt it.”

She looked past him at the room.

“Ruth?”

“No.”

“Down the octave?”

Ruth considered this with half a biscuit in her mouth. Then she put the remainder on the saucer and reached for her folder.

They stood. Elaine gave them the chord on the piano, then another because the tenors had already wandered away from it.

The passage was slower than the recording. Martin had to take an extra breath. Ruth sang her high phrase down among the altos, with a curiously stout effect. At the bottom E he could hear Peter beside him, and beyond Peter a third bass who had not attended for long enough to know the passage had become controversial.

Elaine held the last chord until it settled.

“Good,” she said. “Now put the cut in.”

Martin drew the brackets with his ruler. Peter watched him.

“Portugal?” he whispered.

“What?”

“Any good?”

Martin put the ruler away.

“Lovely. Bit windy.”
