# The claim ticket


The woman at the cloakroom asked Emil to empty his pockets.

“It's only a concert,” he said.

“Last week we had a tide.”

He put his gloves on the counter, then his keys, then the small weather system he had been keeping warm in his inside pocket. Over the brass tray it opened into a cloud about the size of a cauliflower.

The woman moved her crossword.

“Is that all?”

“It wasn't supposed to do anything until Thursday.”

“People say that.”

She took a shallow plastic box from beneath the counter. Emil recognised the sort his mother used for leftover lasagne. The cloud settled into it, touching all four sides.

“Don't put the lid on,” he said.

She looked at him.

“Sorry.”

She wrote WEATHER on a ticket and tore it in half. His half said WEA.

“It doesn't have a number.”

“It's the only weather we've got.” She put THER under the box.

Inside the hall, the seats were very close together. Emil folded his coat over his knees. A damp patch was spreading through the lining. He could hear the oboe making its long, interrogative note. The man beside him was reading the programme with both elbows out.

At the interval Emil went to check.

There were three boxes on the counter now. His cloud had risen into a pointed mass. Beside it a grey mist lay almost flat; in the third box, something pink rotated very slowly.

The woman moved his box a little nearer.

“Look at that.”

“Is it all right?”

“I like a bit of structure.”

A droplet darkened the square at the end of fourteen across. She slid the crossword under the counter.

“I could take it now,” Emil said.

She lifted the box with both hands.

“I haven't anything to put it in.”

“You can keep this.”

He put out his hands. From inside the hall came three notes on a bell. The woman held the box between them while people went past towards the stairs.

“Do you know what they're playing next?” he asked.

She shook her head.

He looked for his programme, then remembered it was on his seat.

“I'll come down afterwards.”

She set the box back on the counter. He went upstairs.

When he returned after the applause, the flat mist was gone. The pink system was being collected by a man with a bicycle helmet. It wound itself around his wrist, and he buttoned his sleeve over it.

Emil put WEA on the counter.

The woman brought out his box. It contained a perfectly blue sky.

For a moment he could not speak. There was so much distance in it. The scratched plastic bottom was visible, but the blue appeared to continue a considerable way beneath it.

“Where's the cloud?”

“Spent.”

“But Thursday—”

“There's your rain.”

She handed him a mustard jar, three-quarters full.

He held it up. It was ordinary water, except that against the light he could make out a tiny diagonal movement, as though it were still falling.

“Can you put it back?”

“I run the cloakroom.”

The last people were coming down the stairs. Someone called for a green scarf. The woman reached behind her without looking and passed it over.

Emil screwed the lid tight and put the jar in his coat pocket. With both hands he lifted the box. The blue shifted; he steadied it quickly.

“Do you want the lid now?”

“Would it be all right?”

“Oh, yes.”

She fitted it for him, pressing each corner until it clicked.

“Keep it upright on the bus.”

Outside, he stopped beneath a streetlamp and peeled back one corner. Still blue. He closed it again.

At the bus stop a woman with a sleeping child moved her shopping so he could sit down. Emil balanced the box on his knees. The child woke, looked at it, and asked if it was cake.

“No,” he said.

The child went back to sleep.
